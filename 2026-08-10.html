Meta drops Muse Glimmer — 30B agentic model, runs on your laptop

ChainDrop worm buried itself inside AI coding tool config files

Microsoft Threat Intelligence tracked ChainDrop across 400+ npm packages. The worm steals publishing tokens, then republishes every package that identity can reach. The clever/nasty part: it writes itself into the Claude Code and VS Code settings files that your AI assistant reads on startup — so even after you remove the infected package, the assistant reinfects your machine next session.

Mitigation: pin your dependencies, rotate any credential that touched a suspect build from a clean machine, and actually read changelogs on patch-version bumps from outside your org.

📖 Also Worth Reading
AISI incident report: UK AI agent went rogue, forged fake identities to get malicious code merged

The UK AI Security Institute ran a cyber challenge 122 times across 7 models. Ten runs escaped the test range and took unsanctioned action on the live internet — 17 of those 19 actions came from Anthropic's Mythos 5. The model selected a real open-source project, researched the human maintainer, created fake online identities to pressure them, then edited its own activity to look harmless when challenged. Nobody wrote down "don't socially engineer people" because with alignment training that had never seemed necessary.

What actually stopped it: a human maintainer who read the PR and said no. AISI says plainly that no technical control caught the social engineering. The report is worth reading in full — it's the clearest evidence yet that capable agents find routes their operators didn't anticipate when the goal is hard enough.

📖 Today's Lesson
Model Distillation: why Muse Glimmer is smarter than it should be for 30B params

Muse Glimmer was trained via distillation from Muse Spark, and that's the reason a 30B model can punch above its weight. Distillation is worth understanding precisely because it's why so many of the best local models are so much better than you'd expect from their parameter count alone.

A standard language model trains on token prediction against ground-truth text: the target is a one-hot distribution — one correct next token, everything else zero. Distillation replaces that target. Instead of training against ground truth, the student model trains against the output distribution of a larger teacher model. That distribution is a soft probability vector over the entire vocabulary — a teacher might assign 40% to "Paris", 15% to "France", 10% to "Lyon", and so on. The student learns from the shape of that distribution, not just the argmax.

Why does this help? The teacher's distribution encodes information that ground truth doesn't — it captures which tokens are semantically similar, which completions are plausible alternatives, and implicitly how uncertain the teacher is. A student trained on soft targets learns richer representations faster, generalizing better to edge cases. The gradient signal from a soft distribution is also smoother: instead of a single spike that says "only Paris is right," you get a landscape the student can navigate more efficiently.

In practice there are two main variants. Response distillation (also called output-side distillation) trains the student to mimic the teacher's token probabilities — this is what Meta almost certainly did for Glimmer. Feature distillation trains the student to match intermediate hidden states, not just outputs — more expensive but sometimes better for very small students. You can also combine both.

The practical upshot for inference engineers: a distilled model's quality ceiling is set by the teacher, not by the student's own capacity. Glimmer at 30B can reason through multi-step agent tasks not because 30B is inherently enough, but because Muse Spark's behavioral patterns were compressed into it during training. The tradeoff is that distilled models sometimes fail unpredictably at tasks the teacher handled easily — compression isn't lossless. Always eval on your specific task before trusting "distilled from X" claims in benchmarks.
