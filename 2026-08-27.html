📖 Today's Lesson
MoE Expert Routing: How 6B of 125B Parameters Do All the Work

Both models dropped today — Qwen3.8-Flash-Next (125B total / 6B active) and GLM-5.3-Flash (320B total / 18B active) — are Mixture-of-Experts architectures. The numbers look strange until you understand how MoE routing works.

A standard dense transformer activates every parameter for every token. An MoE model instead splits the FFN layer into N "expert" sub-networks. For each token, a lightweight router (usually a small linear layer followed by softmax) scores all experts and picks the top-k. Only those k experts process the token — the rest sit idle. So a 125B-parameter model might activate only 6B parameters per forward pass, matching the compute cost of a much smaller dense model while retaining the knowledge capacity of the larger one.

The router is trained end-to-end with the rest of the model, but it needs careful load-balancing: if every token routes to the same two experts, you get a bottleneck and wasted capacity. Most implementations add an auxiliary load-balancing loss during training that penalizes uneven expert utilization, nudging the router toward spreading load. DeepSeek-V3/V4 goes further with a "no auxiliary loss" approach, using bias terms on the router logits instead to achieve balance without polluting the main loss signal.

For inference, MoE introduces a new problem: expert locality. In a multi-GPU setup, experts are sharded across devices. A token needing expert 7 (on GPU 3) and expert 22 (on GPU 11) requires two all-to-all communication rounds per MoE layer. This is why MoE models are often harder to serve efficiently than their active-parameter count suggests — the communication overhead can dominate at small batch sizes. vLLM and SGLang both have ongoing work on expert-parallel scheduling to minimize this. Something worth watching as these 100B+ MoE models become the community norm.
