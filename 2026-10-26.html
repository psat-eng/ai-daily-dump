OpenAI and Anthropic cut their LLM prices by up to 80% within a few months. Why?

Max Woolf lays out a timeline running from the GPT-5.6 launch in June to GPT-6.1 Sol on Sep 29. It includes an 80% cut to GPT-5.6 Luna on Jul 30, Fable 5.1's 75% cut to cache-read pricing on Sep 1, Opus 5.5 at $4 / $20 / $0.20 per million tokens on Sep 22, and GPT-6.1 Sol at $2 / $10 / $0.10. He argues cache reads make up 96–99% of token spend in coding-agent workloads, so cache pricing matters more than headline input or output rates.

His explanation is competitive pressure: models have become near-commodities that are easy to swap, and open-weight options keep improving. He cites DeepSeek V4.1 Flash, now hosted by 29 providers on OpenRouter, and Kimi K3.1 Pro at $2 / $10 / $0.20. Vercel gateway data in the post shows cost per token at 43.3% of its April 2026 level, while aggregate spend keeps rising because models improve faster than they get cheaper.

Infrastructure · Oct 22 · 358 points on HN Oct 25
How Cerebras deploys trillion-parameter frontier models

Cerebras walks through serving the roughly 3T-parameter Kimi K3 across wafers, each with 44 GB of on-chip memory. At 4-bit weights each of the model's 93 layers is about 16 GB, so one dense-attention layer sits on a wafer alongside its KV cache, while two linear-attention layers fit per wafer because they need no growing cache. By its arithmetic that comes to 59 wafers, connected as a pipeline in which weights stay put and only activations hop between wafers over RDMA at roughly 2 microseconds per hop. Cerebras says this yields 1,000–2,000+ tokens per second.

The post counters the idea that a pipeline runs at batch size one: every stage works on a different request or micro-batch at once. The number of concurrent requests is capped by the on-chip memory left for the dense layers' KV cache. Throughput figures are Cerebras's own.

IBM CEO says open-weight models are now irresistibly good and cheap on-prem

On IBM's Q3 call, CEO Arvind Krishna said open-weight models now run at 10–25% of frontier token cost while being about 90% as effective, up from a benchmark gap of roughly 70 versus 20–30 in 2024. He listed summarising email, examining a contract and answering a customer call among tasks the cheaper models handle as well. Krishna said most enterprises can build useful on-prem capacity in two or three racks but will struggle with kernel engineering, GPU placement and caching, and said IBM will ship software to ease deployment. The 90% figure is his claim, not a benchmark result.

🛠️ Flip Tech Watch
Flip Tech Watch · Oct 25
Cache-read pricing as the lever on agent cost

The pricing analysis above reports that cache reads account for most of the spend in long agent loops and that providers now price them at 1–10% of input. Several providers list cache reads at $0.10–$0.20 per million tokens.

Flip angle: a voice agent re-sends a growing system prompt, knowledge-base excerpts and call history on every turn, which is the same shape as the coding-agent workload described. Logging cache-hit rate per call and keeping the static prompt prefix stable would show how much of per-call cost is cache reads. The post's figures come from coding agents, so the share for short phone calls needs measuring before any build-versus-buy decision.

Flip Tech Watch · Oct 22 · 4 days old
Open-weight models for routine call handling

IBM's CEO named answering customer calls among tasks open-weight models handle at a fraction of frontier cost, and Kimi K3.1 Pro's weights are promised by Nov 4 with more than 20 providers already hosting its predecessor, according to the pricing analysis above.

Flip angle: intents with simple order-status or returns-policy answers could be A/B tested on an open-weight model hosted by a third-party provider, measuring automation rate and escalation reasons against the current model. Vendor and executive claims of "90% as good" are not call-level evidence; the first test should replay a sample of escalated calls and compare outcomes.

🧠 Today's Lesson
Why a dense-attention layer needs a KV cache that grows, and a linear-attention layer does not

In standard (dense) attention, each new token compares itself with every earlier token, so the model keeps each earlier token's keys and values in memory: the KV cache. Its size per token is roughly 2 × layers × KV heads × head dim × bytes. With illustrative numbers of 80 layers, 8 KV heads, a head dimension of 128 and 16-bit values, that is about 320 KB per token, or around 80 GiB for a single 256,000-token request. The cache grows linearly with context length and must be held for every request in flight.

Linear attention replaces the growing list with a fixed-size running summary, a state matrix updated as each token arrives. Memory per request stays constant whatever the context length, at the cost of a lossier summary of distant tokens. Many recent models therefore mix the two: a few dense layers for precise recall and many linear layers for cheap bulk processing. The Cerebras post above describes Kimi K3 with 69 linear and 24 dense layers, and notes that only the 24 dense layers need cache space.

The practical consequence is capacity planning: concurrent requests are bounded by memory left after weights for dense-layer caches, not by total layer count. Hybrid designs raise that bound sharply, which is one reason long-context serving prices have been falling.
