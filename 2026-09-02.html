Inference Engineering
The Efficient Frontier of LLM Inference (Baseten)
Philip Kiely, Baseten · HN: 141 points · Today

Clean conceptual framing that's worth internalizing: inference techniques either move you along the latency–throughput frontier (batch sizing, parallelism strategy) or push the entire frontier outward (kernel optimization, speculative decoding, disaggregation). The distinction matters because frontier-pushing gains compound: 2× from hardware + 2× from software = 4× total, allocatable however you want.

Good section on parallelism: Tensor Parallelism for latency, Expert Parallelism and Attention Data Parallelism for throughput. Also covers MXFP4/NVFP4 quantization as a frontier-pusher (quality tradeoff is surprisingly flat until you push too hard). Ties into their book Inference Engineering for deeper dives.

📖 Today's Lesson
Prefill–Decode Disaggregation: Why Your GPU Cluster Has Two Distinct Jobs

When you send a prompt to an LLM, inference has two completely different phases. Prefill processes all your input tokens in parallel and fills the KV cache — it's compute-bound, burns through matrix multiplications at high intensity, and completes in one forward pass. Decode then generates output tokens one at a time (or a small speculative batch), which is memory-bandwidth-bound: each step fetches the entire KV cache from GPU HBM. These two phases have almost opposite hardware requirements.

The traditional approach runs both on the same pool of GPUs. That works, but it's a permanent compromise: batch sizing that's good for prefill hurts decode latency, and vice versa. P/D disaggregation (prefill–decode disaggregation) solves this by routing each phase to a dedicated worker fleet. Prefill workers get heavy compute (wide tensor parallelism, big batches), decode workers get optimized memory bandwidth (aggressive KV cache management, smaller per-request footprint).

The ratio between prefill and decode workers isn't fixed — it's tuned to match your traffic's input/output token ratio and cache hit rate. High prompt-cache hit rates mean you need fewer prefill workers. Long output sequences (agentic loops, code generation) push you toward more decode capacity. In practice, disaggregation is best for high-volume, latency-sensitive deployments; smaller clusters often don't have enough total GPUs for the separation to pay off. Frameworks like vLLM and SGLang have been adding disaggregation support — it's moving from research curiosity to production primitive.
