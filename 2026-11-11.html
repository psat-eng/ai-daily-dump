48 AI agents comb NASA lidar and flag an "unmapped" Maya civic centre, then a correction lands

Pietro Miotti, a self-described non-archaeologist, had agents convert about 1,200 km² of public NASA lidar strips into images, then had 48 agents inspect them one by one for pyramids, platforms and plazas while filtering out hills, sinkholes and roads. He stresses there was no special classifier: the agents looked at the pictures against a written guide. One hilltop with a levelled plaza and an oval pyramid of at least 8–12 m did not appear in the literature search, and INAH, Mexico's heritage institute, replied that no registered site existed at that location.

A November 11 update reverses the headline: the site had already been mapped in Estrada-Belli et al. 2025, which the agents missed during their literature review. It was a rediscovery, not a discovery. The thread became a case study in both halves: agent swarms are fast at visual triage, and still unreliable at the "has anyone done this before" step.

Leak · Nov 10 · 442 points on HN
Apple's unreleased "Harbor" app would cluster Macs over Thunderbolt 5 into a local inference server

9to5Mac reports that leaker Aaron Perris found a Mac app called Harbor, in private preview under Apple's Claris brand, described as turning a Mac into "a private AI inference server" for open-weight models. The leaked copy says Harbor detects Thunderbolt 5-connected Macs automatically and pools their unified memory to run models larger than any single machine can hold, or to serve more concurrent requests, with in-app model download and no command line. Apple has not announced it, and there is no release date. The story drew 459 comments on Hacker News.

Safety engineering · Nov 9
OpenAI's Control Room: a fail-closed kill switch built into the inference layer

Every agentic research workload at OpenAI now gets a job_id from a central service, and inference engines check that ID before serving each request; once a monitor or human stops a job, further sampling is refused within 35 seconds, a lag OpenAI attributes to an authorization cache it is working to remove. The check lives in the engine rather than in fast-changing workload code, and if Control Room itself goes down, all inference is denied.

Research · Nov 9
"Can a Linear Program Do Weight-Space Programming?"

Nathan Barry revisits MIT CSAIL's result in which GPT-6 Astra edited 0.016% of Qwen3.5-0.8B's weights and lifted MATH-500 from 7.9% to 48.1%, mostly by suppressing thinking loops (93% of base answers hit the 32k-token limit). He argues the edit, which pushes two MLP down_proj layers against a "loop direction" in the residual stream, is a linear program, and reports that a couple of minute-long LP solves match Astra's 8-hour, 28-forward-pass search with 8x fewer changed weights.

Flip Tech Watch · Nov 9
Per-request authorization at the inference gateway as an agent safety pattern

Control Room (see "Also Worth Reading") enforces stop decisions in the one place every request must pass, the inference engine, and fails closed if the control service disappears.

Flip angle: The same shape fits a per-client or per-call kill switch at Flip's LLM proxy: tag each request with client and call IDs, check a cached allow-list, and refuse generation for a client whose config or prompt is pulled mid-incident, falling back to live-agent transfer. For a voice agent, failing closed on every request would be too blunt; the sensible variant fails to escalation rather than silence, and the cache TTL (OpenAI's is the source of its 35-second lag) is the parameter to size against acceptable exposure time.

🧠 Today's Lesson
Tensor vs pipeline parallelism: why a cluster of Macs on Thunderbolt adds capacity more easily than speed

When a model is too big for one device, there are two basic ways to split it. Tensor parallelism cuts every layer's weight matrices across devices, so each device computes a slice of every layer. The slices must be recombined with an all-reduce roughly twice per layer, for every token. A 60-layer model therefore needs over a hundred synchronisations per generated token, which is why tensor parallelism lives on links like NVLink with hundreds of GB/s and microsecond latency.

Pipeline parallelism instead gives each device a contiguous block of layers. Only the activation vector for the current token crosses the link between stages, about 16 KB for an 8,192-wide model in 16-bit precision. That fits easily over Thunderbolt 5 (80 Gb/s, roughly 10 GB/s), which is why Harbor's leaked pitch of pooling unified memory is plausible on consumer cables.

The catch: with a single request, the stages take turns. Device two waits while device one runs its layers, so one conversation is no faster, and often slightly slower, than on a single large machine. The cluster's win is capacity (a model that would not otherwise fit) and throughput, because with several concurrent requests in flight every stage stays busy. That matches the leaked copy's phrasing exactly: "large models and higher concurrency," not lower latency.
