📖 Today's Lesson
MXFP4: Why OpenAI's Jalapeño Chip Thinks in Tiny Floats

The Jalapeño chip's headline number — 13.4 PFLOPs — is in MXFP4, a format that barely existed in mainstream discussion two years ago. Understanding it explains why custom silicon keeps producing PFLOP counts that look impossibly large.

FP4 basics: A standard 32-bit float (FP32) uses 1 sign bit, 8 exponent bits, 23 mantissa bits. FP4 uses just 4 bits total — typically 1 sign, 2 exponent, 1 mantissa. It can represent a tiny range of values with very coarse precision. Multiply two FP4 numbers and you get garbage for most tasks — unless your values are carefully scaled.

Microscaling (MX) is the key fix: MXFP4 (from the OCP MX spec, now in PyTorch and CUDA) attaches a shared FP8 scale factor to blocks of 32 elements. Every 32 weights in a tensor share one scale. This means you're not doing raw 4-bit arithmetic on globally unscaled values — you're doing 4-bit arithmetic on values that have been normalized to a useful range per-block. The precision loss is real but quantifiable, and modern training recipes can compensate for it.

Why it unlocks ridiculous FLOP counts: Compute density scales roughly as 1 / bits² for matrix multiplies. Going from BF16 (16-bit) to FP4 means ~16× more operations per transistor per clock. That's where the 13+ PFLOP numbers come from on silicon that draws 700W — roughly the same envelope as a data center GPU from 2023 doing ~2 PFLOPS of FP32.

The catch: Not all workloads tolerate FP4. Attention QK products, layer norms, and softmax typically need higher precision. Real systems use FP4 for weight-heavy matmuls and fall back to BF16 or FP8 for precision-sensitive ops — so the effective utilization of those 13 PFLOPs on a real model is well under 100%. But even at 30–40% utilization, the throughput gains are significant enough to justify the architectural complexity.
