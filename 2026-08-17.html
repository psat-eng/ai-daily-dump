Today's Lesson
Tensor Parallelism vs Pipeline Parallelism: how inference servers split a model too big to fit on one GPU

When a model doesn't fit on a single GPU — think GLM-5.3's 743B parameters or Kimi K3's 2.8T — you have to split it across devices. There are two fundamentally different ways to do this, and the choice shapes your latency, throughput, and hardware utilisation.

Tensor parallelism (TP) slices individual weight matrices horizontally across GPUs. Each GPU holds a shard of every layer and processes every token simultaneously. The GPUs have to communicate at every layer via an all-reduce to stitch results back together. The upside: every forward pass uses all GPUs in parallel, so TTFT stays low. The downside: you need extremely fast interconnects (NVLink, or InfiniBand at 400Gb/s+) because the all-reduce happens hundreds of times per token. TP is why H100 clusters are wired with NVLink — without it, the communication becomes the bottleneck.

Pipeline parallelism (PP) splits the model by layers: GPU 0 runs layers 1–20, GPU 1 runs 21–40, etc. Each GPU processes a batch stage ("micro-batch") and passes activations forward. Communication only happens at layer boundaries — far less frequent than TP's all-reduce. The tradeoff: GPUs spend time idle waiting for the previous stage (pipeline "bubbles"), and TTFT scales with pipeline depth. PP works better when inter-GPU bandwidth is limited (commodity InfiniBand, cross-node) and when you're optimising for throughput over latency.

In practice, large inference deployments use both: TP within a node (fast NVLink), PP across nodes (slower inter-connect). vLLM and SGLang both support this hybrid. When you see a serving config like --tensor-parallel-size 8 --pipeline-parallel-size 4, that's 32 GPUs total — 8-way TP per stage, 4 pipeline stages.
