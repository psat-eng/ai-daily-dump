PHP2Shell: frontier AI helped turn long-dormant PHP engine flaws into working exploits

Wiz Research reports multiple memory-safety vulnerabilities in core PHP, grouped under two CVEs with severity up to CVSS 9.2, affecting every release back to PHP 5.4. The bugs stem from how PHP handles fatal errors such as out-of-memory conditions, leaving corrupted state that a later request can reuse. PHP shipped patches on October 21.

Wiz says the underlying flaws were decades old but that chaining them into exploits sat at the edge of what human researchers could practically do, and that frontier models let the team build and adapt working exploits across several applications in hours. It credits the PHP team, Calif and OpenAI for discovery, coordination and patch verification. The severity framing, including a comparison to Heartbleed and Log4Shell, is Wiz's own.

Meta Muse's shared-conversation links were discoverable through public DNS records

Ars Technica reports that researchers at Claroc enumerated 6,800 links Muse users had created to share conversations. The links put the conversation title in the subdomain of cf.metaaiusercontent.com, so passive DNS databases recorded them once a recipient opened one. Among the few hundred the researchers opened were a confidential executive-search call prep, a company revenue review, a loan application and a staff vacation calendar.

Claroc says it reported the issue through Meta's bug bounty on October 8 and received no reply. Meta told Ars it has paused the sharing feature while it reviews the report and considers changes to how the links can be discovered.

Open Weights · Oct 21 · vendor case study
Hirundo: Qwen3-Coder produced more hackable apps when the prompt named a US defense contractor

Hirundo generated a user-secrets web service from BaxBench thousands of times with Qwen3-Coder-30B-A3B-Instruct, changing only who the code was said to be for. It reports apps were 19% more likely to be hackable for a US defense contractor than for a Chinese one, and 28% more likely to let any user read others' secrets for a Hong Kong democracy group. Adding a security instruction did not close the gap.

The company says its "Westernized" version, with the model's alignment removed through behavioral unlearning, showed none of the three gaps and matched the original on ten other coding tasks. Hirundo sells unlearning and hardening, the study is its own, and the work builds on earlier CrowdStrike and Booz Allen reports it cites.

🛠️ Flip Tech Watch
Flip Tech Watch · Oct 18 · 3 days old
To Make, or to Buy: The Economics of Decision Models

The author describes "decision models" such as Jev 1.13 on OpenRouter, which return a probability for each of a fixed set of options, a probability-weighted score, or a true/false probability. Because the output is constrained and carries odds, the post proposes a "decision hierarchy" of rules and caches, then decision models, then LLMs, then humans, with each level answering only when confident. It applies Chow's 1970 reject rule: answer when the expected cost of being wrong is below the cost of passing the input down.

Its napkin math, the author's own, prices 400,000 classification queries at about $13 on GPT-5 nano versus $4.2 on Jev.

Flip angle: Intent routing on inbound calls fits the pattern: a cheap classifier handles confident intents, and low-confidence calls fall through to the full voice-agent LLM or a live agent, with the threshold set by the cost of a misrouted call versus an escalation. Caveat: the cost figures are unaudited, and the first test is replaying logged calls to see how well the classifier's confidence tracks actual routing errors.

🧠 Today's Lesson
Abstention and cascades: letting a cheap model say "not sure"

A cascade puts models in order of cost: a small classifier first, a large LLM second, a human last. Each stage answers only when it is confident and otherwise abstains, passing the input down. Most traffic is easy, so most of it never reaches the expensive stage.

The simplest rule is a threshold T on the top class probability p_max: answer if p_max > T, otherwise defer. Raising T means fewer answered inputs and fewer errors among those answered. Chow's rule makes the choice economic: if a wrong answer costs 1 and deferring costs F, answer only when 1 - p_max < F. With F = 0.2 and p_max = 0.6, the expected error cost is 0.4, so defer.

Two catches. First, F is not fixed: it is the next stage's price plus its own error rate times its error cost. Second, the rule only works if the probabilities are calibrated, meaning inputs scored 0.8 are right about 80% of the time. Many classifiers are overconfident, so calibrate on held-out data (temperature scaling is the usual start) and re-check whenever the traffic mix shifts.

The same idea sits behind model routing in LLM products: the hard part is rarely the routing, it is trusting the confidence number.
